Act I · The Pitch

How to tell if you're buying a car or just a car payment

The tiny asterisk next to an advertised car payment hints at a hidden truth. It's left up to the buyer to do the calculations (most don't). So I did them for you. Pull up a chair and pour a coffee.

What you'll see this Saturday
$399/mo*

A new ride. Easy financing. You deserve this.

* For well-qualified buyers. See dealer for details.

Every car ad in America says some version of this. A small monthly number. A friendly verb. An asterisk small enough to completely gloss over. This visualization is about what's on the other side of that asterisk.

Same math. Two different directions. The only difference is whether you find yourself in the driver's seat or along for the ride in the back seat.

If you've ever seen a retirement-account ad — "start at 25, retire a millionaire" — that's the top half. The bottom half is what happens when the same force is making money from you, not for you.

Act II · Meet Jordan

It's a Tuesday.

Jordan is thirty. Just got promoted. Has been driving the same hand-me-down sedan since college and figures it's time. Walks into a dealership. The salesperson is friendly. The brochure is clean. The numbers feel reasonable. Here's what's actually being shown — and what's quietly not.

What the dealership says
$50,326

The all-new 2026 — yours this Saturday.

What gets added before the pen touches the paper
  • Sticker (MSRP) $50,326
  • Sales tax (~7%) + $3,520
  • Dealer doc fee + $899
  • Title & registration + $320
Out the door $55,065

Numbers vary by state, but the pattern doesn't.

The sticker isn't the price — it's the starting point. By the time the pen touches the paper, Jordan has already added almost $4,700 nobody printed on the brochure. That's a tip you never agreed to.

What the salesperson says
$787/mo

We can get you in for under eight hundred a month.

What that $787 is actually buying the bank
  • Down payment $5,000
  • Loan principal $45,326
  • Interest rate (APR) 6.4%
  • Term 69 months
  • Total of payments $54,303
Of which is interest $8,977

The car costs the same. The math just costs more.

~ 19%

$787 above is the average. Almost one in five new car loans now run over a thousand dollars a month — that's not the high end, that's the new high end. The sticker isn't the only cost. It's not even the biggest one.NPR · Oct 2025

Most people only ever check the monthly. $787 sounds doable. $8,977 in interest, paid quietly over six years, is roughly a year of groceries — handed to the bank for the privilege of borrowing money Jordan didn't have.

What never comes up at the dealership
$0/mo

Insurance? That's between you and your provider.

What the lender will require before they hand over the keys
  • Liability only (used, owned outright) $61/mo
  • Full coverage (required while financed) $220/mo
  • Difference forced on you + $159/mo
  • Per year $1,908
Over the 69-mo loan $10,971

The lender owns a chunk of the car until you've paid it off. They insure their chunk by making you insure all of it.

That's about a phone bill every month, for the right to borrow. It doesn't appear on the loan paperwork because it isn't loan paperwork — it's the loan's shadow. If you're financing, you're insuring at full coverage. Always.

Act V · The Tuesday That Didn't Happen

Same Tuesday. Different math.

Rewind to the parking lot. Jordan, thirty, freshly promoted, same hand-me-down sedan. This time, instead of pulling into the dealership, Jordan keeps driving. Pulls up Craigslist. Finds a clean four-year-old Corolla for eight thousand dollars. Buys it. In cash. Same car ad on the radio on the way home. Here's what happens to the math.

Same parking lot. Same groceries. Same roads.

This is the part the dealership never asks Jordan to picture, because they have a financial interest in Jordan not picturing it. A used Corolla isn't a sacrifice. It gets to the same parking lot. The difference between Jordan's used car and the new one isn't transportation — it's an upfront lump of cash plus roughly $946 a month, every month, for the rest of Jordan's working life. About That Asterisk

Remember the $399 at the top?

From the top of the page
$399/mo*

A new ride. Easy financing. You deserve this.

* For well-qualified buyers. See dealer for details.

What the asterisk actually requires
  • A base-trim vehicle, not the SUV in the photo ~ $25,000
  • Top-tier credit (FICO 750+) ~ 4.5% APR
  • A short loan 60 months
  • A meaningful down payment $5,000
  • Tax, title, registration, insurance not included
What the average buyer actually pays $787/mo

$787 − $399 = $388/mo of asterisk. Over 69 months of payments, that's $26,772 the brochure never had room to mention.

The $399 ad isn't a lie. It's an asterisk. To actually pay $399 you have to hit every single one of those conditions — right car, right credit, right down, right term. Miss any of them — which is what happens to the average buyer — and the number climbs to $787. Miss a few more, or pick a slightly nicer trim, or have a less-than-perfect credit score, and you're in the 19% paying over $1,000/mo. Same dealership. Same Saturday. The number on the brochure is the number for someone else.

Frequently Asked

“But what about —”

A lot of the math above gets met with the same handful of objections. Here's where each one actually lands when you do the numbers — including the cases where the math works in your favor. Open whichever ones speak to you.

The stories we tell ourselves
“A new car is more reliable. Won't repairs on a used car cost me more in the long run?”

Reliability isn't really the issue — a four-year-old Toyota or Honda solves it. The average annual repair bill on a five-year-old car is about $548. The year-one depreciation hit on a new car averages around $11,000. So:

Year-1 depreciation on a new car~$11,000
Annual repair budget on a 5-yr-old car$548
Years of repairs to equal that loss20+

You'd need 20+ years of repairs on a used car to break even on the depreciation of buying new. What “new” actually buys is convenience — no surprise bills, no Saturday at the mechanic. That's real value. It's also about ten times the price of the thing it's avoiding.

“But I really need a reliable car for work. I can't risk a breakdown.”

Reliable transportation is a category, not a price tag. A four-year-old Camry or Civic that's been through one careful owner solves the “I have to be at work on Monday” problem identically to a new SUV.

4-yr-old Camry/Civic, good condition$18,000
New SUV (US average)$50,326
The gap, which is not transportation$32,326

The extra $32,000 isn't the car — it's the feeling of newness. Which is real. But it's also thirty-two thousand dollars of feeling.

“I'll keep this car forever, so the depreciation doesn't matter.”

Almost nobody keeps a car forever. The honest US average is about 8.4 years — right when depreciation has done most of its work and the new-car itch returns. Fewer than 14% of owners hold a car past 15 years.

And here's the part that surprises people: even if you really do keep it for twenty years, the depreciation didn't disappear. It happened privately, in the first six years, while you weren't looking at the resale value. The market value drop is a cost whether you sell the car or not — it just becomes invisible if you don't transact.

“I work hard. I deserve this.”

You probably do. Nobody's arguing that. But “I deserve this” isn't a math claim — it's a values claim, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.

It shows up here because it's the only sentence in the entire dealership that doesn't have a receipt. No interest line. No depreciation line. No retirement-trade-off line. Just rhetoric. That's not an accident — it's the easiest sentence to weaponize. Knowing that is most of the protection you need.

“The warranty and free maintenance plan are included. That's real value.”

Yes, the warranty is real. Yes, the included oil changes and loaner program are real. Tot them up:

3-yr bumper-to-bumper warranty value~$1,200
10 yrs of “free” oil changes~$1,800
Loaner program (avg use)~$3,200
Real value of all “free” perks~$6,200
Depreciation paid to get them~$32,000

That's paying full price for a steak dinner to get the free bread basket. The perks are real; they're just not the deal you think they are.

“Trade-in value protects me. I'll just trade it in when I'm ready.”

Trade-in value doesn't get you out of the depreciation — it just resets the cycle. You hand the dealer back a car that's lost most of its value over the last six years, and they help you start the next loan at peak depreciation again.

The average American driver does this 5–7 times over a working life. Each cycle has its own ten-thousand-dollar depreciation bite. The dealership is delighted to do it every six years for the rest of your life. That's not a hedge. That's a subscription.

When the math actually works the other way
“Wait — isn't all debt bad?”

No. Debt is a tool. Sometimes it's the right one. The question is whether your investment return outpaces your borrowing rate, and whether the thing you bought is holding or losing value. Three quick examples:

Mortgage at 3.5% vs. S&P at ~10%works for you
Auto loan at 6.4% vs. S&P at ~7–10%coin flip — until depreciation
Credit card at 22%catastrophic

A 3% mortgage on a house that appreciates while the market does 10%? The math works for you. A 22% credit card balance? You can't outrun that. The car loan is interesting precisely because the rate alone is borderline — it's the depreciation that tips it.

“Why does depreciation tip the auto loan from break-even to a loss?”

Because you're paying interest on something that's losing value at the same time.

A mortgage is debt against an asset that mostly holds or appreciates. Even if your rate is meaningful, the underlying thing tends to gain value. The combined math is usually positive.

A car loan is debt against an asset that's actively melting. Even if the interest rate alone is close to break-even against the market, the asset is shedding 15–20% of its value in the first year. The borrowing isn't the problem on its own. The borrowing combined with a depreciating asset is what makes the math go the wrong way.

“Why 7%? Isn't the S&P historically higher than that?”

7% is the historical real (inflation-adjusted) average return of the S&P 500 over the long run. 10% is the nominal average — what your statement would say before adjusting for what dollars actually buy.

For retirement projections, real returns are what matter, because you'll be spending future dollars on future-priced goods. Using 7% means “in today's purchasing power, here's what your money becomes.” If you want a more optimistic projection, slide the calculator up to 10%. If you want a more conservative one, slide it down to 5%. The point of the slider is that your assumptions are visible, not hidden.

Compound interest works for you when your investment outpaces your debt. It works against you when your debt outpaces your investment — or when what you bought with the debt is losing value. That's the only sentence in this whole piece you actually have to remember.
